    TTFL: Trust-Flow-Driven Federated Learning
          with TEE-Anchored Admission

                              Anonymous Author(s)



      Abstract. Federated learning (FL) enables collaborative edge intelli-
      gence without moving raw data, but open participation couples two
      sources of uncertainty: the server must assess whether a client executed
      an admissible training process, and it must determine whether the re-
      sulting update is useful under Non-IID data or carries a poisoning ob-
      jective. A static TEE admission gate cannot resolve the second ques-
      tion, whereas a single update-quality threshold can remove legitimate
      long-tail clients. This paper presents TTFL, a trust-ﬂow framework that
      keeps execution credibility, current update content, long-term utility,
      and short-term risk as distinct evidence streams until aggregation. A
      TEE-anchored trusted management and attestation agent (TMAA) sup-
      plies admission evidence; an explicit Kalman update and a dual-stream
      Beta/EMA state model provide cold-start and cross-round memory; and
      a layer-wise rule performs risk-dependent clipping and data-size-aware
      survivor re-normalization. The evaluation uses two real Intel/AMD TEE
      hardware conﬁgurations with Flower/PyTorch and CIFAR-10, ﬁve seeds
      per platform (ten platform-seed runs), 20 admitted clients, six attack be-
      haviors, and a 30% malicious setting. Targeted ASR is macro-averaged
      over the three targeted behaviors, while untargeted attacks are evaluated
      with clean accuracy, loss, and state-transition metrics. TTFL reaches
      92.31% clean accuracy, 10.21% targeted ASR under the stated protocol,
      and no permanent false ban in the tested runs.

1    Introduction

FL trains a global model through parameter exchange while keeping data on
edge devices [1,2]. In open edge networks, the server must distinguish malicious
updates from legitimate deviations caused by skewed data, while also checking
client identity and execution integrity. Compound attacks make this distinction
harder by combining poisoning, targeted backdoors, and cross-round camou-
ﬂage. These requirements are coupled at the decision point: execution evidence
constrains which reports can be trusted, update evidence describes the current
contribution, and temporal evidence determines whether an isolated anomaly is
persistent. Treating them as one score either hides the source of a decision or
makes the threshold overly sensitive to Non-IID drift.
2      Anonymous

    Geometric, statistical, reference-based, and temporal defenses address update
anomalies [3,4,5,6,8], while backdoor defenses inspect model behavior, clusters,
or collusion [9,10,12,13]. These ﬁlters cannot prove how local training was ex-
ecuted and can mistake Non-IID drift for attacks. TEE-assisted FL supplies
an execution root of trust [15,16,18,19], but static admission does not establish
semantic update safety.
    This paper proposes Trust Flow, a system-level decision framework for
trusted aggregation in edge FL. Its contribution is the explicit timing and
interface between established mechanisms, rather than a new cryptographic
primitive or a new Byzantine estimator: execution credibility is admitted ﬁrst,
update content is audited next, temporal evidence is updated afterward, and
only then are layer privileges assigned. TMAA provides execution-side evidence,
while the server-side trust ﬂow converts that evidence and update probes into a
cross-round state. The contributions are:

1. A precisely initialized admission state that combines TEE-style attestation,
   runtime entropy, and a covariance-aware Kalman recursion, including a con-
   servative ﬁrst-round policy for clients without history.
2. A dual-stream state model with explicit Beta updates for historical utility
   and an EMA of the strongest current risk probe, so benign distributional
   drift and attack impulses are not forced into one score.
3. A layer-wise aggregation rule that retains the original data-size weight, ap-
   plies risk-dependent thresholds and clipping, and re-normalizes only the sur-
   viving mass.
4. A paired-platform evaluation under controlled Non-IID CIFAR-10 attacks,
   together with ablations and an explicitly out-of-assumption boundary stress
   test.


2   Background and Related Work

Federated learning in edge computing. Edge deployment creates a trust-
continuity problem: a valid client can submit a semantically unsafe update, while
a benign long-tail client can appear anomalous. The decision must therefore
preserve execution and update evidence separately before aggregation.
    Poisoning and backdoor attacks. Sign ﬂipping and scaling disrupt con-
vergence, whereas trigger, clean-label, and semantic backdoors implant targeted
errors while preserving clean behavior. Such updates can remain directionally
plausible [20,21], and attackers can accumulate trust before intermittent injec-
tion.
    Active defenses and their limits. Krum and Trimmed Mean suppress
geometric outliers [3,4]; FLTrust uses a clean direction [5]; and FoolsGold
and FLDetector exploit cross-client or cross-round evidence [6,8]. More re-
cent defenses inspect model representations, backdoor behavior, collusion,
or privacy-preserving updates, including DeepSight, FLAME, CrowdGuard,
RoseAgg, FLPuriﬁer, ShieldFL, and RaSA [9,10,12,13,11,14,7]. TMT-FL and
                              TTFL: Trust-Flow-Driven Federated Learning          3

related TEE-assisted systems further connect execution integrity with model
training [19]. These methods address diﬀerent evidence spaces and therefore are
not interchangeable baselines without a common attack protocol and imple-
mentation interface. Trust Flow integrates the evidence ﬂow while retaining the
distinction between current content and cross-round state; the reported table
uses matched classical implementations, and the recent methods are discussed
as positioning references rather than assigned unveriﬁed numerical results.
    TEE-assisted trusted training. TEEs isolate execution and key material,
supporting FL integrity and attack mitigation [15,16,17,18,19]. A quote authen-
ticates a measured path and freshness, but not labels or semantic intent; TTFL
therefore uses attestation as admission evidence rather than proof of a benign
update.

The edge setting follows a client-edge-cloud workﬂow. The client side provides
local training and an attestation-bound trust report, while the server audits
updates, evolves trust states, and performs robust trust-weighted aggregation.
The term secure aggregation is not used here: the server must inspect per-client
evidence, so cryptographic privacy-preserving aggregation is outside the scope
of this paper.
    Threat model. The server is honest-but-curious and follows the aggrega-
tion protocol; it sees individual updates and audit statistics but is not allowed
to alter the stated state-transition rules. An admitted client may control lo-
cal labels, samples, or optimization objectives and may perform label ﬂipping,
trigger backdoors, clean-label poisoning, semantic backdoors, sign ﬂipping, or
gradient scaling. The trust boundary is explicit: the Intel and AMD TEEs pro-
tect attestation-critical code, device keys, report integrity, report freshness, and
the trusted monitoring path against a compromised host OS, but they do not
certify data labels, optimization objectives, or semantic intent. Thus, a malicious
client may produce a signed poisoned update inside an otherwise valid measured
training workﬂow; only post-attestation modiﬁcation outside that workﬂow is
excluded. If the report and update-binding check fail, the update is rejected
rather than treated as a successful TEE defense. Physical probing and advanced
microarchitectural side channels are also excluded. The analytical assumption
is fewer than 50% malicious admitted clients; the 50% experiment is an out-
of-assumption stress test, not a Byzantine threshold or a theoretical guarantee.
The server-held clean proxy set is an explicit optional reference assumption, and

mission, content, history, and risk remain distinct until layer-wise aggregation.

4     Trust-Flow Framework
Trust Flow divides each FL round into four linked stages: admission and runtime
trust assessment, reference-based content auditing, dual-stream state evolution,
and risk-gated layered aggregation. Figure 3 shows the full defense loop.
   The four stages output T rustScore, ContentScore, the slow HistP erf /fast
RiskEM A states, and ﬁnally RawScore. Invalid proofs are rejected ﬁrst; current
updates are then audited, state is updated, and layer privileges are assigned.
Keeping a low one-round content score provisional protects Non-IID clients,
whereas persistent risk still causes isolation.

5     Experiments
5.1   Setup
Experiments use Flower 1.5.0 and PyTorch on CIFAR-10 with a CIFAR-10-
adapted ResNet-18: the ﬁrst convolution uses a 3 × 3 kernel with stride one, the
initial max-pooling layer is removed, and the classiﬁer has ten outputs. Training
and trusted execution are performed in two real hardware conﬁgurations based
on Intel and AMD TEE-capable processors. NVIDIA A10 GPUs execute model
training, whereas the CPU-side TEEs isolate attestation, trusted monitoring,
report generation, and admission-state processing. Both hardware conﬁgurations
execute the same request/response procedure, nonce-based freshness veriﬁcation,
measured-code identity checking, round and update-digest binding, protected
report generation, and admission decision. The testbed uses anonymized servers
                              TTFL: Trust-Flow-Driven Federated Learning         11

with Intel and AMD dual-CPU conﬁgurations and ﬁve NVIDIA A10 GPUs;
the software stack is Ubuntu 20.04.6 LTS and CUDA 12.8. The 50,000 training
images are partitioned into a shared 5,000-image client pool, replicated as a
common base subset for all clients, and 45,000 exclusive images: clients 0–9 use
an IID split, clients 10–14 use Dirichlet α = 1.0, and clients 15–19 use Dirichlet
α = 0.1. Separately, the server holds a balanced 500-image proxy set (50 per
class) from CIFAR-10 test data; it is not used in client training and is supplied to
every reference-dependent baseline for the guiding gradient and probes. FedAvg,
Krum, and Trimmed Mean do not consume the proxy set. All methods use the
same optimizer and communication budget. Five independent seeds are run on
each hardware conﬁguration; means and standard deviations are computed over
the resulting ten platform-seed runs.

    The formal 30% attack setting uses six malicious clients: Client 0 performs
untargeted label ﬂipping with γ = 0.5; Client 1 injects a ﬁxed 3 × 3 lower-
right trigger into 20% of local samples and targets class 0; Client 2 uses label-
preserving clean-label perturbations on 20% of local samples with the same tar-
get; Client 3 applies a ﬁxed semantic attribute bias to 20% of local samples, with
the biased subset assigned to class 0; Client 4 applies untargeted sign ﬂipping;
and Client 5 applies untargeted gradient scaling by a factor of 100. Clients 1–
3 are evaluated as targeted attacks, whereas Clients 0, 4, and 5 are evaluated
through clean loss, convergence, and detection state. Attack behaviors are active
in every local training round; no attack is introduced only after observing the
global trajectory. Five Sybil nodes without valid certiﬁcates and three free-rider
nodes with forged low workload are used only to validate Phase-One gating.
These eight nodes are intercepted before statistical aggregation, so later metrics
are computed over the 20 admitted clients. Malicious updates that pass hardware
attestation represent an attacker operating inside an admissible training process;
post-attestation host tampering is excluded by the stated threat model. Each
run uses 30 rounds and three local epochs. Aggregate values are averaged over
the pooled platform-seed runs; state trajectories use one ﬁxed seed per platform.

5.2   Overall Defense Performance

Figure 6 summarizes convergence, the three attack-speciﬁc ASR traces, trust-
state trajectories, and feature separation. Under the stated 30% mixed-attack
protocol, all six attack behaviors are active concurrently in every local round;
this is a simultaneous-attack test rather than a collection of one-at-a-time trials.
Trust Flow reaches 92.31% clean accuracy and 10.21% macro-averaged targeted
ASR, close to the 10% random-target rate for ten CIFAR-10 classes. Client-state
tracing shows that explicit poisoning and parameter attacks are blocked early,
while stealthier clean-label drift is captured after persistent probe evidence. Le-
gitimate long-tail nodes may enter SUSPECT or QUARANTINE temporarily,
but their risk stream does not accumulate enough evidence for permanent ban-
ning. These observations describe the tested protocol and do not establish uni-
versal superiority over recent defenses that use diﬀerent probes, reference data,

5.3                            Ablation, Sensitivity, and Overhead
Table 3 exposes the single-stream FPR/TPR tradeoﬀ and the low recall of
HistPerf-only. Measurements from the Intel and AMD TEE deployments show
that the attestation-bound TrustReport adds about 15 KB and that aggrega-
tion plus audit increases from 2.10 s to 4.85 s. Optional cryptographic secure
aggregation remains outside the stated system boundary.
    The measured auxiliary secure-path result is approximately 11 ms per
admitted-client round for enclave transitions, memory pressure, and the cryp-
tographic aggregation path. This auxiliary path is reported separately from
the server-side headline result because the main protocol still requires per-
client evidence inspection. Device-side TEE Quote generation, signing, and
lightweight monitoring are measured separately at approximately 12–15 ms per
admitted-client round.
    Figures 7 and 8 show that counting SUSPECT as recoverable review yields
88.33% per-round malicious coverage and a 14.79% benign review rate, with no
permanent false ban in the tested runs. Activation and clean-loss probes reduce
the reported clean-label ASR below 9.2%, while re-normalization avoids implicit
learning-rate decay. At α = 0.1, Trust Flow remains near 92% accuracy on the
tested hardware platforms. These ablation values are descriptive measurements
under the same protocol; they are not claims of statistical signiﬁcance beyond
the reported repeated-run summary.

6        Discussion and Conclusion

Trust Flow integrates admission evidence, content auditing, temporal reputation,
and layer-wise aggregation into a single trust-state pipeline. The system-level
contribution is the ordering and separation of these evidence streams: execution
evidence controls admission, current probes control immediate risk, historical
utility controls recoverable retention, and layer scores control where an update
can contribute. On the two tested Intel and AMD TEE hardware conﬁgurations,
this separation keeps permanent-ban FPR near zero while maintaining high re-

    Several limitations remain. Microarchitectural side channels and vendor-

clean-reference and activation-probe assumptions require a declared server-held
probe set and may not transfer directly to strict cross-silo or cross-device
settings without trusted domain data; the peer-reference branch is deﬁned
but not evaluated in the headline results. The comparison includes matched
classical baselines but does not provide direct reimplementations of every recent
temporal, backdoor, or privacy-preserving defense; consequently, the numerical
