\begin{abstract}
    Federated learning (FL) must assess both the execution that produced an update and the update's effect on the global model. Under heterogeneous data, a useful long-tail client can look anomalous, whereas an attacker can build a favorable history before submitting a harmful update. Trust Flow separates hardware-anchored admission, current content evidence, historical utility, and recent risk before layer-wise aggregation. A Kalman filter handles runtime evidence; decayed Beta evidence and a risk EMA drive persistent state transitions. Layer gates, clipping, and sample-size-weighted normalization control each surviving update. The reported Flower/PyTorch experiments use CIFAR-10, ResNet-18, a replicated shared training pool, and six concurrent attack classes. Across two platforms with five runs each, the experimental record reports 92.31\% clean accuracy and 10.21\% macro targeted attack success rate at 30\% malicious participation, with no final benign bans. A separate implementation trace provides attack-specific evaluation and gate-selection records. The evidence distinguishes final retention from participation and identifies the validation limits of the specified aggregation and calibration rules.

\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
    Federated learning, edge computing, trusted execution environment, risk modeling, layered aggregation, backdoor defense
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

FL enables edge devices to train a shared model through parameter exchange while keeping their raw samples local \cite{kairouz2021flsurvey,mcmahan2017fedavg}. In an open edge deployment, however, the server must assess both the execution that produced an update and its effect on the global model. This assessment becomes difficult under Non-IID data: a legitimate long-tail client may repeatedly deviate from a reference direction, whereas an attacker may build a favorable record before submitting a harmful update. A single reputation score makes these cases difficult to separate. Tightening its threshold can remove useful heterogeneous contributions, while allowing past performance to compensate for current anomalies can delay the response to an attack. The resulting challenge is to preserve evidence of long-term contribution without allowing that evidence to conceal short-term risk.

Existing defenses use geometric, statistical, reference-based, or temporal evidence to flag unusual updates \cite{blanchard2017krum,yin2018byzantine,cao2021fltrust,fung2020foolsgold,fldetector2022}. Backdoor-oriented methods additionally examine model behavior, learned representations, clusters, and collusion \cite{deepsight2022,flame2022,crowdguard2024,roseagg}. Such tests say little about how local training was executed, and the same tests can interpret Non-IID drift as an attack. TEE-assisted FL contributes a hardware-rooted execution boundary \cite{r1_tee_integrity,xu2021distributed,r5_tee_mitigating,lu2025tmt}; admission based on that boundary still leaves the semantic safety of an admitted update unresolved.

The proposed \emph{Trust Flow} policy addresses this tension by maintaining historical utility and short-term risk as separate states. Historical utility records contribution, while current risk can restrict an update despite a favorable earlier record. These states connect TEE-based execution admission, content auditing, and layer-wise aggregation. Their separation permits different responses to weak contribution and persistent risk, but does not by itself identify the cause of a deviation. The contributions are:
\begin{tightenumerate}
    \item A dual-stream state representation gives historical contribution and short-term risk separate roles, supporting reversible restrictions and persistent-risk escalation.
    \item A state-to-aggregation policy links recoverable suspicion, persistent-risk escalation, and layer-specific weighting. Content quality can lower a contribution without, by itself, forcing irreversible exclusion.
    \item An evaluation separates final-ban outcomes, reversible review, and layer selection, combining the reported repeated-run comparison with a separately scoped implementation trace.
\end{tightenumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\textbf{Federated learning in edge computing.} Local training and server aggregation allow clients to collaborate without transferring raw samples, but leave the server dependent on the evidence accompanying each update. Heterogeneous data distributions complicate that assessment because a useful long-tail contribution may differ from the majority. Execution provenance and update quality consequently serve different purposes: the former establishes how an update was produced, while the latter determines how it should contribute to learning.

\textbf{Poisoning and backdoor attacks.} Sign flipping and gradient scaling disrupt optimization by changing update directions or magnitudes. Trigger, clean-label, and semantic backdoors instead seek targeted errors while preserving plausible main-task behavior, so their updates can remain close to an expected direction \cite{bagdasaryan2020backdoor,wang2019neuralcleanse}. Across rounds, an attacker may also accumulate a favorable history before injecting a harmful update, making current behavior and past contribution potentially conflicting sources of evidence.

\textbf{Active defenses and their limits.} Krum and Trimmed Mean remove geometric outliers \cite{blanchard2017krum,yin2018byzantine}, FLTrust weights updates against a clean direction \cite{cao2021fltrust}, and FoolsGold and FLDetector use cross-client or cross-round evidence \cite{fung2020foolsgold,fldetector2022}. DeepSight, FLAME, CrowdGuard, RoseAgg, FLPurifier, ShieldFL, and RaSA inspect representations, backdoor behavior, collusion, or privacy-preserving updates \cite{deepsight2022,flame2022,crowdguard2024,roseagg,flpurifier,shieldfl,wang2025rasa}. TMT-FL and related systems connect execution integrity to model training \cite{lu2025tmt}. These approaches observe different evidence spaces. Trust Flow places an interface between them: attestation determines execution eligibility, privacy-aware checks expose bounded statistics, content probes inspect the current update, and the two temporal streams decide future privileges. One observation can therefore alter a layer weight, a recoverable state, or a persistent state only when the corresponding rule is satisfied. The experiments use classical methods as matched aggregation baselines and stateful variants to isolate this temporal interface.

\textbf{TEE-assisted trusted training.} TEEs protect execution and key material and have been used to support FL integrity and attack mitigation \cite{r1_tee_integrity,xu2021distributed,zhang2025rppfl,r5_tee_mitigating,lu2025tmt}. A quote authenticates the measured path, freshness, and update provenance. TTFL combines that hardware evidence with privacy-aware verification and cloud-side semantic auditing before assigning aggregation privileges.

\section{System Model and Threat Assumptions}

Figure~\ref{fig:system_arch} presents the client-edge-cloud environment, consisting of a central aggregation server $\mathcal{S}$ and $K$ edge clients $\mathcal{C}=\{c_1,\ldots,c_K\}$. In round $t$, admitted clients train from the distributed model $W^{(t-1)}$ and submit updates $\Delta W_k^{(t)}$ for auditing and aggregation. The server then computes $W^{(t)}=W^{(t-1)}+\mathrm{Agg}(\{\Delta W_k^{(t)}\})$, with the global learning objective
\begin{equation}
    \min_W \mathcal{L}(W)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_k\mathcal{L}_k(W),\quad
    p_k=|\mathcal{D}_k|/\sum_j|\mathcal{D}_j|.
\end{equation}
The client side performs local training and supplies an attestation-bound report, while privacy-aware verification exposes the declared data and runtime statistics needed for admission and audit. Raw samples remain local. In the stated system boundary, the CPU-side TEE protects the measured admission agent, device keys, report integrity, nonce freshness, and admission state; GPU training memory and the semantic correctness of labels, samples, objectives, and updates are outside that boundary. Thus an attacker may produce a signed poisoned update inside an otherwise measured training process, while post-attestation host tampering is excluded. Using the submitted update and this evidence, the cloud server evolves the client's trust states and determines its aggregation privilege. Because these decisions require individual updates and audit evidence, cryptographic secure aggregation is not enabled in the evaluated protocol.

\textbf{Threat model.} The boundary in Fig.~\ref{fig:system_arch} separates report integrity from update semantics. The server is honest-but-curious and follows the aggregation protocol. It can see individual updates and audit statistics, but it does not change the stated state-transition rules. An admitted client may alter labels, samples, or optimization objectives and may conduct label flipping, trigger backdoors, clean-label poisoning, semantic backdoors, sign flipping, or gradient scaling.

The original protocol reports Intel- and AMD-capable TEE platforms. The
protected path binds the identity, round, nonce, global-model digest, and
received update digest before signing. The server verifies the quote/key
binding, signature, freshness, and digest consistency before semantic audit.
This sequence does not establish the integrity of externally collected gradient,
loss, or instruction statistics. Their collection must be protected or supported
by separately authenticated instrumentation. Per-platform TEE mechanisms and
instrumentation are not specified in the available experiment record. The CPU-side TEE protects attestation-critical code, device keys, report integrity, nonce freshness, and the binding among client identity, round number, global-model digest, update digest, and signing key. It does not authenticate labels, samples, optimization objectives, semantic intent, or GPU memory. Consequently, the six attacks below may operate inside a valid measured training process; tampering with the update after attestation or attacks on the TEE implementation itself are excluded. Physical probing and advanced microarchitectural side channels are also excluded. The assumed malicious fraction is below 50\%; the 50\% experiment is a boundary stress test rather than evidence of a Byzantine guarantee. The main experiments use a 500-sample server proxy for reference and probes. The peer-reference branch only supplies a direction when that clean direction is unavailable; clean-loss and activation probes still require the proxy and are not silently treated as reference-free.

\textbf{Design goals.} Trust Flow aims to retain useful heterogeneous
contributions while restricting persistent risk. Execution provenance, current
content, historical utility, and recent risk retain separate meanings until
layer-wise aggregation. Recovery and low false-ban rates are empirical goals,
not consequences of attestation alone.

\section{Trust-Flow Framework}

Each FL round passes through four linked decision stages: admission and runtime assessment, reference-based content auditing, dual-stream state evolution, and risk-gated layered aggregation. Global model update and feedback close the five-phase loop shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}.

The stages in Fig.~\ref{fig:framework} pass progressively different evidence to the aggregation decision. Admission and runtime assessment produce $TrustScore$, content auditing produces $ContentScore$, and state evolution accumulates evidence in $HistPerf$ and $RiskEMA$. Their combination yields $RawScore$, which controls layer privileges. The server first removes clients that fail the admission proof, then scores the remaining updates against the reference and peer group. Keeping contribution and risk distinct allows a weak single-round contribution to reduce influence without immediately ending a benign client's opportunity to recover.

\subsection{Admission and Content Auditing}

Each admitted client passes a TEE admission check. Let
$M_{\mathrm{attest},k}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether the quote, nonce freshness,
code identity, round identifier, current global-model digest, update digest, and
signing-key binding are valid. The runtime observation is defined from the standardized behavior sequence
$\mathcal{X}_k^{(t)}=\{\Delta\mathrm{grad},\Delta\mathrm{loss},
\mathrm{instr\_dist}\}$. For a declared histogram with $B$ bins and probabilities $v_b$,
$H=-\sum_b v_b\log v_b$ and $H_{\max}=\log B$. The entropy is normalized as
$h_k^{(t)}=H(\mathcal{X}_k^{(t)})/H_{\max}\in[0,1]$. The observation and
measurement-noise terms are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    R_k^{(t)}&=\exp(\lambda h_k^{(t)}),\\
    Z_k^{(t)}&=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(
    w_aM_{\mathrm{attest},k}+(1-w_a)(1-h_k^{(t)}),0,1\right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $w_a=0.7$ and $\lambda=1.0$. For a valid proof, $Z=1-0.3h$:
entropy both increases measurement uncertainty and applies a soft reliability
penalty to the observation mean. It is not a maliciousness label. The scalar random-walk Kalman recursion is
\begin{equation}
    T_k^{-,(t)}=T_k^{(t-1)},\quad P_k^{-,(t)}=P_k^{(t-1)}+Q,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    K_k^{(t)}&=\frac{P_k^{-,(t)}}{P_k^{-,(t)}+R_k^{(t)}},\\
    T_k^{(t)}&=T_k^{-,(t)}+K_k^{(t)}
        \left(Z_k^{(t)}-T_k^{-,(t)}\right),\\
    P_k^{(t)}&=(1-K_k^{(t)})P_k^{-,(t)} .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $Q=0.01$, $T_k^{(0)}=1$, and $P_k^{(0)}=0.25$. The admission score is
\begin{equation}
    TrustScore_k^{(t)}=M_{\mathrm{attest},k}
    \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{T_k^{(t)}}{1+P_k^{(t)}},0,1\right).
\end{equation}
A newly attested client starts in NORMAL; first-round evidence may reduce its
current aggregation privilege but cannot directly create an irreversible blacklist.

\textbf{Frozen peer statistics.} Let $\mathcal D_t$ be valid current submissions
from clients not previously blacklisted. Before updating states, define
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathcal R_t=\{k\in\mathcal D_t:\;&s_k^{(t-1)}\in
\{\mathrm{NORMAL},\mathrm{SUSPECT}\},\\
&RiskEMA_k^{(t-1)}<\tau_s\}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
This revision restricts shared reference and norm statistics to $\mathcal R_t$.
Quarantined updates are audited against those frozen statistics for recovery
but do not enter them. Peer comparisons for client $k$ exclude $k$ itself.
If required peer or proxy evidence is absent, the audit is marked incomplete:
the client's temporal states are held and its update is not aggregated.
The original experiments must be checked for the same reference-set policy.

The server next constructs a reference direction. In the reported protocol,
$g_{\mathrm{root}}=g_{\mathrm{root\_clean}}$ is obtained from a server-held
balanced proxy set of 500 CIFAR-10 test samples (50 per class), separate
from client training. The direction must have the sign of a model increment,
for example $-\eta_{ref}\nabla_W\mathcal L_{proxy}(W)$ with $\eta_{ref}>0$,
because updates are applied as $W+\Delta W$. The exact reference optimizer and
the proxy/evaluation split identifiers remain run-configuration requirements.
If a clean direction is unavailable,
the fallback is the normalized weighted mean of updates in $\mathcal R_t$, with
$\omega_k^{\mathrm{ref}}=TrustScore_k(1-RiskEMA_k^{\mathrm{prev}})^2$.
This fallback changes only the reference direction; the clean-loss and activation
probes still require proxy inputs. For each update, content quality combines
reference and peer consistency:
\begin{align}
    S_{\mathrm{contrib},k}
      &=\max\!\left(0,\alpha_1+\alpha_2
        \cos(\Delta W_k,g_{\mathrm{root}})\right),\\
    \rho_{kj}
      &=\max\!\left(0,\alpha_1+\alpha_2
        \cos(\Delta W_k,\Delta W_j)\right),\\
    S_{\mathrm{consist},k}
      &=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal R_t\setminus\{k\}}TrustScore_j\rho_{kj}}
        {\sum_{j\in\mathcal R_t\setminus\{k\}}TrustScore_j+\varepsilon},\\
    ContentScore_k
      &=\frac{(1+\beta_{\mathrm{fusion}}^2)
        S_{\mathrm{consist},k}S_{\mathrm{contrib},k}}{D_k},\\
    D_k&=\beta_{\mathrm{fusion}}^2S_{\mathrm{consist},k}
        +S_{\mathrm{contrib},k}+\varepsilon.
\end{align}
Here $\alpha_1=0.6$, $\alpha_2=0.4$, and $\beta_{\mathrm{fusion}}=2.0$.
The score describes current update quality; it is not itself a permanent
reputation decision.

\subsection{Dual-Stream State Evolution}

\textbf{Cold start and first-round evidence.} At $t=1$, $HistPerf_k^{(0)}$ and $RiskEMA_k^{(0)}$ represent initial conditions rather than accumulated client behavior; the runtime trust estimate and covariance likewise start from their filter initialization. In the reference-assisted experimental setting, the server can construct $g_{root\_clean}$ without a client history. Once the first local updates arrive, runtime reports, reference alignment, peer consistency, and the available risk probes provide current-round evidence. These checks do not require a previously established reputation: they assess the first submitted update, while the two temporal streams begin accumulating evidence for later decisions.

A low content score can reflect either a useful client's skewed data or a harmful update. Since those cases require different participation decisions, Trust Flow retains historical utility and short-term risk separately. Let $q_k^{(t)}\in[0,1]$ denote normalized content quality. With the coefficients in Table~\ref{tab:setup} and nonnegative trust weights, $ContentScore_k^{(t)}$ already lies in this interval and serves directly as $q_k^{(t)}$. The unit-evidence form of the decayed Beta model adds $q_k^{(t)}$ to positive evidence and $1-q_k^{(t)}$ to negative evidence:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \alpha_k^{(t)} &= \beta_h\alpha_k^{(t-1)}+q_k^{(t)},\\
        \beta_k^{(t)} &= \beta_h\beta_k^{(t-1)}+1-q_k^{(t)}.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $0<\beta_h<1$ is the history retention factor, distinct from the
negative-evidence mass, and the reported protocol initializes
$\alpha_k^{(0)}=\beta_k^{(0)}=1$, so $HistPerf_k^{(0)}=0.5$. The historical
utility is
\begin{equation}
    HistPerf_k^{(t)}=\frac{\alpha_k^{(t)}}{\alpha_k^{(t)}+\beta_k^{(t)}+\varepsilon}.
\end{equation}
Decay allows later observations to revise the contribution history rather than preserving early evidence indefinitely.

After its initial transient, the history channel retains evidence across rounds. For a legitimate long-tail client, low similarity can lower aggregation privilege while leaving later rounds available for recovery. The risk channel instead retains recent warnings through $RiskEMA_k^{(t)}=\beta_rRiskEMA_k^{(t-1)}+(1-\beta_r)Risk_k^{inst}$, where $Risk_k^{inst}$ is the maximum current probe. Taking the maximum preserves a strong warning across probe channels; maintaining risk separately prevents a favorable history from altering that risk estimate. The state-transition rules then determine whether the evidence warrants review, quarantine, or permanent exclusion.

The separation changes how borderline clients are treated. A client with weak content quality but no rising risk can remain in the system with reduced influence, giving later rounds an opportunity to clarify whether the deviation was benign. A client with favorable historical utility but rising attack risk instead faces restricted aggregation privileges. Low historical utility can lead to temporary soft isolation, while the risk stream records sustained anomalies separately from the contribution deficit.

The two streams meet only when computing aggregation privilege:
\begin{align}
    RawScore_k^{(t)} & = (TrustScore_k^{(t)})^\alpha\nonumber                                      \\
                     & \quad\times(ContentScore_k^{(t)})^\beta(HistPerf_k^{(t)})^\gamma\nonumber \\
                     & \quad\times(1-RiskEMA_k^{(t)})^p .
\end{align}
The resulting state machine distinguishes NORMAL, SUSPECT, QUARANTINE, and
BLACKLIST (Table~\ref{tab:state_transitions}). Historical utility does not cancel
the risk estimate. However, geometric deviation affects both content quality
and risk; separate states therefore do not imply independent evidence or
guaranteed discrimination between heterogeneity and attacks.

The current-round states enter the reported aggregation decision. Evidence is
computed first, the state transition is applied second, the candidate set is
formed third, and layer filtering and aggregation are performed last. A newly
attested client can therefore lose current-round influence without being
permanently banned; BLACKLIST becomes a gate on subsequent rounds.

Let $u_k^{(t)}=1-HistPerf_k^{(t)}$ and $r_k^{(t)}=RiskEMA_k^{(t)}$.
The thresholds $(\tau_h,\tau_s,\tau_q,\tau_b)=(0.26,0.64,0.80,0.90)$
refer to contribution deficit, persistent review risk, instantaneous quarantine
risk, and persistent blacklist risk, respectively.

The instantaneous quarantine branch applies to clients entering the round in
SUSPECT, as in the original state rule. For NORMAL clients, instantaneous risk
still affects the current risk EMA and score. To make the transition order
explicit, this revision specifies at most one transition per submitted round,
with escalation taking precedence over recovery. Recovery is QUARANTINE
$\to$ SUSPECT or SUSPECT $\to$ NORMAL. Counters are local to the starting state,
reset on a state change or a failed condition, and advance only on valid
submissions. Missing submissions freeze historical evidence, risk, and counters.
BLACKLIST excludes the current update and rejects future submissions; it is
irreversible within a run. These timing conventions require checking against
the implementation that produced the retained results.

\subsection{Risk-Gated Layered Aggregation}

Backdoor effects may concentrate in deeper layers even when shallow features
retain useful heterogeneous information. Layer sensitivity sets both the score
gate and clipping radius. With $L$ layers and $l=1,\ldots,L$, the original
sensitivity components are
\begin{align}
 S_{priv}^{(l)}&=\exp(-\tau_p l/L),\\
 S_{util}^{(l)}&=\frac{\|g_{root}^{(l)}\|_2}
 {\max_m\|g_{root}^{(m)}\|_2+\varepsilon},\\
 S_{sec}^{(l)}&=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{|\mathcal T_t|}
 \sum_{k\in\mathcal T_t}\cos(\Delta W_k^{(l)},g_{root}^{(l)})\right).
\end{align}
Here $\mathcal T_t$ contains aggregation candidates with previous risk below
$\tau_s$; if empty, the original rule uses the aggregation candidates for
these statistics. If neither set contains an update, the layer remains unchanged.
$C_{base}$ is the median update norm over the same nonempty set. A zero median
is handled by leaving the layer unchanged. The indexing and numerical
stabilizers, including $\varepsilon_c$, must be recorded in the run configuration.

The privacy term decreases with depth, the utility term reflects the relative reference-gradient norm of the layer, and the security term measures deviation from the reference direction. The combined sensitivity is
\begin{equation}
    S_{total}^{(l)}=w_pS_{priv}^{(l)}+w_u(1-S_{util}^{(l)})+w_sS_{sec}^{(l)},
\end{equation}
where $w_p,w_u,w_s$ are the nonnegative component weights. The resulting sensitivity determines both the admission threshold and clipping radius:
\begin{equation}
    \theta^{(l)}=\mu_{base}+\lambda_sS_{total}^{(l)},\qquad
    C^{(l)}=\frac{C_{base}}{S_{total}^{(l)}+\varepsilon_c}.
\end{equation}
A layer showing stronger suspicious drift therefore has a higher entry threshold and a smaller clipping radius. Here $C_{base}>0$ is the base update-norm scale, while $\varepsilon_c>0$ stabilizes the denominator. Layers that remain close to the reference retain more capacity for benign heterogeneity.

\textbf{Audit eligibility and aggregation eligibility.} A quarantined client
may supply recovery evidence without entering the frozen peer reference.
Let $\mathcal E_t\subseteq\mathcal D_t$ contain submissions with a complete
audit and $s_k^{(t)}$ denote the resulting state. The aggregation candidates are
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal A_t=\{k\in\mathcal E_t\mid
    s_k^{(t)}\in\{\mathrm{NORMAL},\mathrm{SUSPECT}\}\}.
\end{equation}
SUSPECT retains conditional eligibility under the score and layer gates, whereas QUARANTINE and BLACKLIST have no aggregation weight. State transitions are applied before forming $\mathcal A_t$: a quarantine decision removes the current update, and the current-round score is used for the remaining candidates. Recovery restores eligibility only after the state transition has occurred.

The round therefore proceeds from valid submissions to current evidence, state updates, candidate selection, and finally layer filtering and aggregation. The surviving clients are $\Phi_t^{(l)}=\{k\in\mathcal A_t\mid RawScore_k^{(t)}\ge\theta^{(l)}\}$, written as $\Phi^{(l)}$ below for brevity. For a nonempty survivor set, their weights are re-normalized:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \tilde{w}_{k}^{(l)}        & =\frac{p_k\,RawScore_k^{(t)}}{\sum_{j\in\Phi^{(l)}}p_jRawScore_j^{(t)}+\varepsilon}, \\
        \widehat{\Delta W}_k^{(l)} & =\frac{\Delta W_k^{(l)}}{\max(1,\|\Delta W_k^{(l)}\|_2/C^{(l)})}.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The final layer update is $\Delta W_{global}^{(l)}=\sum_{k\in\Phi^{(l)}}\tilde{w}_{k}^{(l)}\widehat{\Delta W}_k^{(l)}$. When $\Phi^{(l)}$ is empty, the layer is left unchanged; no client is backfilled across the layer gate. This rule prevents an empty gate from being bypassed by selecting the largest raw score, and a quarantined or blacklisted client therefore never contributes through a fallback. The data-size factor $p_k$ retains sample-size information in the credibility-adjusted aggregation, while filtering and clipping mean that the original unfiltered objective is not claimed to be preserved exactly. For survivor mass $Z_l=\sum_{j\in\Phi^{(l)}}p_jRawScore_j$, the normalized weights sum to $Z_l/(Z_l+\varepsilon)$. This compensates for removed mass up to the denominator stabilizer; it is score normalization, not a simplex projection.



Layer-wise control allows the same client update to receive different treatment across the network. When a semantic backdoor mainly alters deep features, sensitivity-dependent thresholds and clipping can constrain those layers while allowing more of a consistent shallow-layer update to contribute. Re-normalization addresses a separate optimization effect: removing clients also removes aggregation weight, which would otherwise reduce the update magnitude merely because filtering occurred. The normalized survivor weights separate that magnitude effect from the intended clipping operation.

\paragraph{Cold-start gate bound.} With the stated initialization and the
most favorable first-round evidence, the equations imply
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
P^{(1)}&=0.26/1.26,\quad HistPerf^{(1)}\leq1.9/2.8,\\
RawScore^{(1)}&\leq(1+0.26/1.26)^{-3}\sqrt{1.9/2.8}\\
&\approx0.46922.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Thus $S_{total}^{(l)}>0.05769$ rules out first-round survivors under
$\theta^{(l)}=0.4+1.2S_{total}^{(l)}$. These are analytical consequences of the
reported parameters, not measured gate frequencies. The threshold range can
also exceed one, above every possible raw score. Removing the original
highest-score fallback therefore changes an operationally significant branch;
per-layer survival and skipped-update counts are needed to validate it.

\paragraph{Interpretation of the history stream.} The decayed Beta evidence has a
finite effective mass and therefore behaves as a startup-corrected exponential
smoother: early observations have larger gain, while later evidence is retained
with factor $\beta_h$. Its first-observation gain is $1/2.8$, whereas the risk
EMA gain is $0.15$; the history stream is slower only in the steady-state
sense (gain approaching $0.1$). Bounded observations keep states bounded;
recovery additionally requires subsequent evidence to satisfy the recovery
conditions. Neither boundedness nor separate state storage guarantees recovery.

\subsection{Risk Probe Computation}

All instantaneous risk channels are mapped to $[0,1]$ before fusion. For a
candidate update, the geometric and amplitude probes are
\begin{align}
 r_{\mathrm{grad},k}^{(t)}
 &=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(
 \frac{1-\cos(\Delta W_k,g_{\mathrm{root}})}{2},0,1\right),\\
 r_{\mathrm{amp},k}^{(t)}
 &=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(
 \frac{\|\Delta W_k\|_2}{\operatorname{median}_{j\in\mathcal R_t}\|\Delta W_j\|_2+\varepsilon}
 -1,0,1\right).
\end{align}
The clean-loss probe uses the server proxy:
\begin{equation}
    r_{\mathrm{probe},k}^{(t)}
    =\operatorname{clip}\!\left(
     \frac{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{proxy}}(W+\Delta W_k)
           -\mathcal L_{\mathrm{proxy}}(W)}
          {\delta_{\mathrm{clean}}},0,1\right),
\end{equation}
where $z_j=\max\{0,\mathcal L_{\mathrm{proxy}}(W+\Delta W_j)-
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{proxy}}(W)\}$ is computed on trusted calibration updates and
$\delta_{\mathrm{clean}}=\max\{\varepsilon_\delta,\operatorname{median}_j(z_j)+
3\operatorname{MAD}_j(z_j)\}>0$, with $\varepsilon_\delta>0$ and
$\operatorname{MAD}(z)=\operatorname{median}|z-\operatorname{median}(z)|$.
The positive-part calibration is a revised specification; its calibration
updates and scale must be supplied before attributing measured results to it.
The activation-drift probe follows the original softmax-normalized activation
definition, with $\mathcal H(A)=\operatorname{softmax}(\operatorname{vec}A)$
on the same proxy inputs and the same monitored representation:
\begin{equation}
 r_{\mathrm{trigger},k}^{(t)}
 =\operatorname{clip}\!\left(
 \frac{\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\mathcal H(A_{\mathrm{base}})
 \Vert\mathcal H(A_k)\right)}
 {\delta_{\mathrm{trigger}}+\varepsilon},0,1\right).
\end{equation}
Here $\delta_{trigger}>0$ is the original trusted activation-drift scale.
The monitored layer, calibration updates, and numeric scale are unresolved
configuration details; a 32-bin histogram is not assumed. The probe does not
require knowledge of the attacker's trigger. Peer disagreement uses the
original weighted mean of pairwise cosines:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\bar c_k&=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal R_t\setminus\{k\}}TrustScore_j
\cos(\Delta W_k,\Delta W_j)}
{\sum_{j\in\mathcal R_t\setminus\{k\}}TrustScore_j+\varepsilon},\\
r_{peer,k}&=\operatorname{clip}(1-\bar c_k,0,1).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Cosines with a zero-norm argument are assigned zero in the revised definition.
The instantaneous risk is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 Risk_k^{\mathrm{inst}}&=\max\{r_{\mathrm{grad},k},r_{\mathrm{amp},k},
 r_{\mathrm{probe},k},\\
 &\qquad r_{\mathrm{trigger},k},r_{\mathrm{peer},k}\},\\
 RiskEMA_k^{(t)}&=\beta_rRiskEMA_k^{(t-1)}
 +(1-\beta_r)Risk_k^{\mathrm{inst}}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $\beta_r=0.85$. The maximum preserves a strong warning while the common
range bounds each channel but does not equalize false-alarm probabilities.
At zero cosine, the direction probe is $0.5$ while the peer probe can be $1$.
Per-channel distributions for benign IID, benign heterogeneous, and malicious
updates are therefore needed to assess the maximum fusion rule.

\subsection{Assurance Composition and Reference Role}

The evidence sources complement one another because each resolves a different uncertainty. Attestation and privacy-aware verification establish execution provenance and bounded statistics for admission, while cloud auditing examines the submitted update against reference and peer evidence. Temporal states retain the distinction between a transient deviation and persistent risk. Passing the measured execution path therefore leads into semantic auditing; it does not bypass it. A single low-similarity observation can reduce contribution without causing permanent exclusion, whereas sustained direction or probe risk can still drive quarantine and later blacklisting through the state machine.

A clean validation gradient supplies a stable reference when available, while the peer-reference branch constructs a guiding direction from admitted updates when it is unavailable. Reference agreement contributes to content quality alongside peer consistency; historical contribution and recent risk then influence participation. The reference is therefore one input to auditing rather than a substitute for the dual-stream policy.

\subsection{From Separate States to Participation Decisions}

The distinction between historical contribution and recent risk matters most for clients near a participation boundary. A client with low utility but low risk may receive reduced influence or reversible review, allowing later evidence to clarify whether its deviation was benign. A client with substantial past utility and rising risk instead faces restrictions despite that history. Persistent high risk is required for irreversible blacklisting, so the same threshold need not serve simultaneously as a contribution filter and a permanent-exclusion rule.

Let $B_k$ collect the fixed admission, content, and history factors in
$RawScore_k=B_k(1-r)^p$. For $p>0$ and $0\leq r<1$,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \frac{\partial [B_k(1-r)^p]}{\partial r} & =-pB_k(1-r)^{p-1}\leq0, \\
        r                                        & =RiskEMA_k^{(t)}.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
At $r=1$, the score is zero. This statement concerns the unnormalized score with the other factors fixed; layer selection and normalization determine the update that is eventually applied. SUSPECT and QUARANTINE are recoverable states, allowing later evidence to revise a temporary restriction. Low similarity alone is therefore not a one-round permanent-ban trigger, but a sustained risk signal can still cause exclusion under the explicit persistence rules.

The empirical evaluation must distinguish the state policy from its evidence inputs. A temporal-policy comparison requires identical probes and a matched tuning budget; a probe ablation requires the same state policy. Final-ban outcomes alone do not establish contribution or recovery during training.

\subsection{Final Bans, Review, and Contribution}

A final-ban rate uses a client-level denominator. A review rate uses eligible
client--round events. Neither establishes that a client contributes nonzero
aggregation weight. Table~\ref{tab:ablation} keeps these outcomes separate.
The first three final-ban pairs are retained under the author's interpretation
and the original manuscript label; their underlying event counts and averaging
rule remain to be verified. Missing review results are marked N/R and are not
inferred from final outcomes.



The reported Trust Flow final retention is 100\%, consistent with zero final
benign bans, while its reported review FPR is 14.79\%. These values do not imply
zero review false alarms or 100\% per-round detection. Recovery should be
assessed by the fraction of eligible benign submissions receiving positive
weight, quarantine duration, and rounds until the first positive contribution
after quarantine. Malicious cumulative aggregation weight before isolation
is a separate exposure measure. The local trace supplies layer-selection
counts, but neither final retention nor those counts supplies applied weights
or benign quarantine-recovery times.

The retained single-stream values describe a threshold tradeoff, but the
records do not establish equal evidence inputs and tuning budgets across
variants. A same-evidence comparison should include history-only, risk-only,
single-stream, and dual-stream policies, evaluated at comparable benign
restriction levels. The current values cannot establish general superiority
of two stored states over a tuned single-stream policy.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Discussion and Conclusion}

Trust Flow connects execution admission, current content, temporal evidence,
and layer-wise influence through separate historical-utility and risk states.
The revised specification makes recoverable restrictions, permanent exclusion,
and aggregation eligibility distinct. It also avoids bypassing a failed layer
gate when no update survives. Sustained geometric disagreement can still drive
exclusion, and benign recovery requires subsequent favorable evidence.

The original record reports 92.31\% accuracy, 10.21\% macro target ASR,
and zero final benign bans for 30\% mixed attacks. Its scope is CIFAR-10 with
a large shared pool, a server proxy, and malicious identities in the IID group.
The separate trace makes layer selection and two targeted evaluation
denominators inspectable without expanding the scope of the main comparison.
Matched validation of the explicit empty-survivor and calibration rules,
run-level statistics, actual contribution and recovery, shared-pool sensitivity,
and platform-specific TEE instrumentation remain open evaluation requirements.
